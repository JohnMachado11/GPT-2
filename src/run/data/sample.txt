The lighthouse stood at the edge of the world, where the land gave way to the sea.
Every night the keeper climbed the winding stairs, lit the great lamp, and watched
the dark water for ships. The light turned, and turned, and turned, sweeping a slow
circle across the waves. Sailors far from shore saw the beam and knew that home was
near. They steered toward the light, and the light kept them safe.

The keeper was an old man with steady hands. He had tended the lamp for forty years,
through calm nights and through storms. When the wind screamed and the rain came
sideways, he did not sleep. He fed the flame and trimmed the wick and kept the glass
clean, so the light would never fail. A keeper who lets the light fail is no keeper
at all, he liked to say. The sea does not forgive a dark tower.

One winter a great storm rolled in from the north. The waves climbed the rocks like
grey hands reaching for the tower. The old keeper lit the lamp and stood his watch.
Out in the dark he saw a small boat tossed upon the water, its sail torn, its crew
crying out. He turned the light upon them and held it steady, and by that light the
boat found the narrow channel between the rocks and came at last into the quiet bay.

In the morning the storm was gone. The sea was calm and bright, and the rescued
sailors climbed the hill to thank the old man. He only smiled and pointed to the
lamp. It was not me, he said. It was the light. Keep the light, and the light keeps
you. Then he climbed the winding stairs again, to clean the glass and wait for night.

As the years went on, the keeper grew older, and his hands were not as steady as
before. So one spring a young woman came from the town to learn the work. She was
quick and brave, and she loved the sea, but she did not yet know the patience the
light demanded. The keeper taught her slowly. This is not a job of one wild night,
he said. It is a job of ten thousand quiet ones. The light must turn whether the sea
is angry or calm, whether anyone is watching or not.

She learned to trim the wick so the flame burned clean and even. She learned to climb
the stairs in the dark without a lamp of her own, counting each step by heart. She
learned to read the sky, to feel a storm in the wind before the clouds arrived, and
to keep the lens polished so the beam reached far across the water. Night after night
she stood the watch, and night after night the light turned, steady and sure.

When the old keeper could no longer climb the stairs, he gave her the great brass key
to the lamp room. Keep the light, he told her, and the light will keep them. She took
the key and she kept her promise. And on clear nights the sailors far from shore still
saw the slow bright beam sweep across the waves, and they steered toward it, and they
knew that home was near.
